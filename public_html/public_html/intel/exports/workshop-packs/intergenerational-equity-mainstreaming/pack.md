## 5. Four futures

> **Projection may be outdated**: trend gained 190 signals since authoring

### no change — Rhetoric without teeth

Intergenerational-fairness language stays a framing device in speeches and strategy documents, but budgets, mandates and laws are unchanged. The vocabulary spreads while the distribution of resources between generations holds where it is.

### marginal — Soft duties appear

A handful of governments attach non-binding future-generations tests to budgets and impact assessments. Compliance is patchy and reversible, but a shared methodology begins to form and early adopters cite each other.

### adaptive — Institutionalised checks

Future-generations commissioners, statutory well-being duties and generational impact assessments become standard machinery in several OECD states, surviving changes of government and reshaping how major fiscal and environmental decisions are justified.

### radical — Constitutional standing

Intergenerational equity acquires enforceable status — constitutional clauses, binding duties and courts willing to strike down decisions that offload cost onto the unborn. The principle reorders budgeting rather than annotating it.

**What to watch:**
- (adaptive) A second national government legislates a binding generational-impact test on budgets
- (radical) A high court voids a policy citing intergenerational-equity duties
- (no_change) Commissioners created in one term are quietly defunded in the next
